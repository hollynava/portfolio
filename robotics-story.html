Making a building-scale robot operable by anyone

How I turned expert-only operation of ICON's 3D-printing construction robots into a guided, human-in-the-loop system, and pushed it toward autonomy, while the product was still being invented.

Scroll to walk through it ↓
The starting point

A neglected experience that fell apart on the slab.

When I joined, the operator interface had been designed by software engineers without UX support. It functioned but poorly in the field: washed out in direct sunlight and screen glare, targets too small for gloved hands, and complex navigation that assumed you already knew the machine. Running a print meant memorizing the tool, not using it, and multiple operators were required due to the complexity of the system.

The research

I went to the slab to find what actually broke.

I shadowed operators in real conditions (sun, heat, dust, gloves, noise) and ran usability testing on the interface itself, trying different access points and interaction approaches to see what held up under load and what quietly failed. After that the problems weren't opinions, they were observed.

The redesign

Rebuilt for the hand, the eye, and the noise.

I redesigned the operator experience around field reality, cutting cognitive load at every step. The interface stopped fighting the environment and started working with it.

Large-format, high-contrast design system: a variant of the component library built to stay legible in glare and usable in gloves.
Design across senses: screen, haptics, lights, and sound working together for attention management and safety, so critical cues land even when no one is looking at a screen.
Navigation and surfaces: flexible surfaces that open when needed and tuck away when not, with shortcuts to key screens.
Consolidated monitoring: unified alert patterns and visual gauges in place of scattered readouts and text-based errors.
Full-screen print paths: the plan rendered big and legible, so an operator can read the build at a glance.
A moving target

Designed while it was still being invented, across very different machines.

Throughout my time at ICON, I designed across products in active R&D, over multiple generations and two very different form factors: the Vulcan single-story gantry and the Titan multi-story arm. As the design evolved, two key approaches helped keep it coherent while the ground kept shifting.

Service Controls: a low-lift place to drop in new controls for testing and validating features quickly, accessible, but out of the way during normal, production-ready operations.
Shared product patterns: interaction patterns designed once and reused across products, so each new machine felt like the same tool instead of a relearn.
The features

Autonomy, built up one feature at a time.

Before it was one system, it was a set of features. Each solved a real operator problem and nudged the machine toward more autonomy while keeping a person in control. These came first, and each one was pointing at something bigger.

Routines: packaged operations you can start with one tap and reach anytime, organized into a menu grouped by print phase.
Auto-play: an operator-controlled switch for whether the machine prints the next item on its own. Autonomy you can turn on or off.
User prompting: pulling attention exactly when a manual or non-automated step is needed, and not a moment before.
The core idea

It all came together as task-based construction.

Those features were pointing at one idea, and it became the keystone: translating construction itself into discrete tasks. Together with the software engineers, we defined a system to translate each design element and process into a dependency-based task-graph that we could surface as on-screen tasks with embedded guidance to support operators whenever they needed it. Routines, prompts, and auto-play became parts of that flow rather than separate tools, and expert knowledge was now built into the sequence instead of living in someone's head.

The ecosystem

One operator's screen, wired into the whole project.

The operator tool was never an island. I connected it to the products around it so the field, the office, and the data stayed in sync.

Plan module as the bridge to operations: a digital site plan that assigns selected designs (and their specific print paths) to a designated printer, and pulls design updates straight from the Design module into the digital instructions for the machine.
Placement translation: mapping where a design sits in the 3D environment relative to the physical machine, so print placement is accurate, with additional adjustment enabled from detailed scanning.
Scheduling: configuring shift schedules and staffing for realistic print-time estimates, with a preview of the print build-up.
Data sharing: task progress, performance, and material consumption shipped back to Plan for PM and CM visibility of project status, and to Foundry for deeper analysis and reliability work.
Shared components: one set of 3D models and patterns reused across the ecosystem, for accuracy and faster development.
Fleet: a management product for inventory, configuration settings, and deployments across machines.
The collaboration

The design lived at the seams between teams.

None of this worked in isolation. I sat between disciplines and translated in every direction.

Mechanical engineers: turning new hardware capability into system and operator behavior, targeting what to automate and when, and shaping issue-resolution processes.
Operators: grounding the work in real conditions and the non-robotic duties they juggle, and respecting their attention limits, frustrations, and potential hazards.
Embedded and firmware engineers: turning machine signals into human alerts, and raw errors into resolvable conditions.
Industrial designers: partnering on embedded screens and control placement, and on lights and sound interactions.
Software engineers: turning architectural designs into tasks a person could follow, plus 3D interfaces and scheduling and machine complexity.
Product stakeholders: from architects and construction-manager workflows to support engineers and data analysts.
Envisioning what's next

Once the work was tasks, it could be spoken.

With operation expressed as text-based tasks, I began prototyping where it could go next. This was direction I set and explored, not something that shipped while I was there, but clarified where the foundation was built to go.

The north star

Toward one person running many machines.

Every task, every automated step, every remote view was a piece of the same goal: expertise that scales without more bodies on every site. The direction held even as the hardware kept changing underneath it.
