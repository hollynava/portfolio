Making a building-scale robot operable by anyone

How I turned expert-only operation of ICON's 3D-printing construction robots into a guided, human-in-the-loop system — designed live, while the product was still being invented.

Scroll to walk through it ↓
The stakes

A robot that could print a home — but couldn't run without a crew of experts babysitting it.

When I joined, every build needed multiple operators and an ICON support engineer on site. The knowledge to run a print lived in people's heads and binders, and onboarding took months. For a company trying to mass-produce housing, that's a hard ceiling on ever reaching real customers.

The complication

I was redesigning the cockpit while the plane was still being built — and flown.

The product was in active R&D and live production at the same time. Every decision had to serve two masters at once: a dependable tool operators could trust on a real jobsite today, and a fast-moving platform absorbing brand-new hardware and capabilities every few weeks. I designed for both — stable and legible where operators needed certainty, flexible where the science was still moving.

The reality

Outdoors. Glare. Gloves. A machine you can't look away from.

I did the research in the field, not from a comfortable desk — watching operators work in real conditions to find where contrast, glove-sized targets, and cognitive load actually broke the experience. Developers had been making UX calls without design; the interface couldn't keep up with hardware changing under it.

Design craft

Built for glare and gloves, not an office monitor.

High-contrast visuals, larger hit targets, simplified navigation, and a large-format variant of the design system — carried across two hardware generations, Block 3 → Block 4.

The insight

"The real problem wasn't a better screen. It was translating a machine's plan into a human's next step."

Under the hood, the system does automated task-graph traversal of a building design. I partnered closely with the engineers building it — mapping the sequencing and operator hand-offs for brand-new automated behaviors like cable feeding and smart cleaning, and exploring agent-based interaction with the software team (goal-oriented action planning). The aim never changed: shape how each new capability would actually work with an operator, not just be handed to them. An AI-to-human translation layer, designed alongside the people building the machine.

The system

Expert-only operation became a guided, task-based flow.

The tool walks operators step by step — upcoming tasks, live status, history — instead of relying on memory. Structural, machine, procedural, and conditional tasks, orchestrated into clear, reliable steps with context-rich 3D, 2D, and camera views.

Product judgment

Turning easy-to-miss, expensive tasks into obvious ones.

Refills, blade changes, cleaning steps — each could cost 30-plus minutes of delay. I built a prompting and alert system that surfaces them as clear, timely actions, protecting continuous operation and reducing costly stalls.

The workflows

Every print, broken into steps a newcomer could follow.

Task orchestration presents the plan as executable, guided steps — removing guesswork and translation from plan to execution. Positioning and localization, quality checks, and integrated support live right where the work happens.

Automation

As the machine got more autonomous, it had to become more legible — not less.

I explored how AI could advance the interaction: multi-modal by design — speak, touch, type, show; see, hear, feel. I built a flexible framework for the shift from manual operation toward operator-as-monitor — smart prompts, real-time status, and sound and lighting cues that carry in noisy, outdoor conditions. The machine clearly telegraphs what it's doing and what it will do next, mimicking human communication so trust and control stay with the person. Wake it with a phrase, confirm with one — "Hey Titan" … "Good to go."

Safety by design

A machine this big has to announce itself — in light, sound, and touch.

Autonomy raises the stakes: people work close to a machine that moves on its own. I partnered with the industrial design lead to give Titan a consistent language of status lighting and sound — telegraphing what it's doing and what it's about to do — and designed a haptic layer into the operator's device, so critical cues land even when no one is looking at a screen. A fuller, more human experience, and a safer one: the machine acknowledges the people around it and never hides its intent.

Proximity

One operator. Many machines. From the slab to a home office.

The same system flexes to where you are — up close with physical UI, sounds, and lighting; at a distance over mobile and Bluetooth press-to-talk; or out of view from a desk with a headset. Interaction zones from At Slab and At Printer to Remote Support and Print Command.

Visibility

The first real-time window into the field.

Project and construction managers got near-real-time productivity and performance metrics they'd never had — reducing reliance on separate tracking tools and manual reporting.

Operable by a small team, with fast onboarding — and a path to customers.

What's next

Toward one person running many machines — remotely, by voice.

Remote access, multi-machine control, and voice operation point at a future where expertise scales without more bodies on every site — supported by an early Fleet system for remote diagnostics, configuration, and support, built for deployment beyond ICON-run jobs.

"It wasn't just about improving usability — it was about shaping a system that could evolve alongside the technology, the operators, and the future of construction itself."

How I work on problems like this: I think in systems and move between the vision and the pixel — defining where a person should hold the decisions and where the machine should assist, then designing that seam hand-in-hand with the engineers building it. On something still being invented, the craft is holding a dependable experience steady while the ground keeps moving underneath it.
