About

I'm a design-based product leader and systems thinker who creates clarity in complex spaces, most often where the physical and digital meet. I'm most useful on tough problems that are fragmented and ambiguous, finding the throughline that makes the path forward clear.

I think in systems: I work out how a whole ecosystem fits together, then move fluidly between the high-level architecture and the low-level detail, from setting the product vision and defining how the pieces connect, to designing the exact interface someone uses and managing development and delivery. I build products that work for users, the business, and the teams building them, products that can scale and bridge to future capabilities.

I do my best work with a direct line to the people I'm building for: users and decision-makers within reach, and with an organization that has few silos and a mission that matters.

How I work with AI

AI as a force multiplier, not a substitute for expertise

I use AI heavily and deliberately, but always on top of real product judgment and craft. On its own it drifts, and it can lull you into false confidence if you don't have the underlying expertise to know where it's likely to go wrong. What makes it powerful is clear direction, trusted data, consistent rules, and the ability to identify the failure modes.

At ICON, I used Claude regularly to generate and pressure-test design concepts, comb through lengthy research for undiscovered patterns, manage a design system at scale, and build working prototypes that communicated vision far faster than mockups. When missing team capacity was impacting the team, I built an automated product-management system that processed dozens of meeting transcripts a week, tracked my roadmap against Jira ticket status, and suggested sprint plans and generated progress reports.

My thoughts on product

A few things I've come to believe

Velocity is a poor substitute for quality

Moving fast is frequently necessary but not sufficient, and it can mean going the wrong way faster. Great teams ship consistently, but hold a quality standard they won't drop under pressure, and use short experiments to strengthen and validate direction, never as shortcuts to bypass rigor.

Trust past behaviors

While people can very clearly articulate what they think they want or will do, past behaviors are much better predictors of how they make decisions. When creating new products, it's critical to watch what people actually do, and find examples of how they've behaved in similar past situations, and avoid asking customers to anticipate future behaviors.

Leverage the team's superpowers

It pays off to spend time discovering what teammates are genuinely best at and energized by, and find opportunities to work to those strengths. Allowing role flexibility makes for happier teams and a truer read of actual capabilities and gaps, rather than forcing people into rigid roles or reinforcing organizational silos that don't account for how unique humans truly are.

You have to call it as you see it

Learning from what doesn't work is as valuable as learning from what does, likely more. But you have to call a spade a spade, and do it early. It's detrimental to force-fit a solution, or keep a complex one alive too long, because someone fell in love with it before it was proven. Teams that speak up honestly, with the freedom to try and fail fast are the ones that discover the breakthroughs.
