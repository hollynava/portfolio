BuildOS — a product ecosystem for 3D-printed construction

ICON isn't just building a robot, it's trying to invent a new way to build, where design, hardware, materials, and services all have to come together at once. The software to tie those pieces into one experience didn't yet exist. Rather than patch the disconnected tools one by one, I identified a single unified ecosystem, built on a common data spine and shared product components, and owned the strategy and design to make it real: BuildOS, the digital thread that carries a customer from design through construction, adopted as the company's official product direction.

The problem

Inventing a new way to build, all at once

ICON isn't really a robotics company; it's an attempt to reinvent home construction end to end: 3D-printing hardware, new materials, architectural design, and the services around them. The hard part is that none of it works in isolation. For the full experience to exist, every piece has to come together at the same time, in a young company still writing the playbook as it went.

The software was where that convergence had to live, and it wasn't there yet. Design, planning, and printing ran as disconnected internal tools that didn't share data, so nothing could be reliably automated, measured, or handed to an external customer. And between designing a home and building it on a real site sat an entire gap, site planning and construction management, that no tool owned at all.

What I did

Architected one ecosystem from a pile of disconnected tools

Audited the whole tool landscape

I audited every internal and external tool across ICON's design-to-build process and mapped where friction, duplication, and scale risk actually lived, rather than where the roadmap assumed they did.

Modeled a shared data spine

I modeled the end-to-end workflow, from design to planning to print to sale to ownership, into a single shared data spine that every module reads from and writes to, so the whole system could be automated and measured instead of stitched together by hand.

Defined the missing middle

The biggest gap wasn't a weak feature, it was an absent one: nothing connected designing a home to building it on a real site. I defined Plan as a new module for site planning, scheduling, and construction management, the connective tissue between the design and print workflows that carries a customer all the way through.

Pressure-tested the concepts with customers

With the concepts developed, I drove a 27-interview customer research program across developers, builders, general contractors, and specialty trades to pressure-test the direction, then sharpened the modules and priorities from what came back.

Authored the strategy, roadmap, and PRDs

I wrote the ecosystem strategy, an 18-month phased roadmap, and the module PRDs: problem framing, OKRs, scope tradeoffs, and cross-functional alignment across product, engineering, hardware, and architecture.

Set the direction and designed the details

I owned the ecosystem strategy and also personally designed the Print operator app end to end, along with the concepts and design direction for the other modules, so the high-level vision and the actual shipped screens never drifted apart.

One ecosystem, five parts: three customer-facing products, two supporting platforms, all sharing one data spine. Plan is the piece that hadn't existed before, the connective module that carries a customer from a chosen design through site planning and construction management to a finished print.

Outcome

Adopted as the company's product direction

The architecture was adopted as ICON's official product direction. All five modules are now in internal use, at varying stages of completeness, and the customer-facing ones are scoped to ICON's Titan external launch, targeted for Q1 2027.

The bet underneath it: in a business where the full experience only appears when hardware, materials, and services converge, software is the thread that holds them together and moves a customer through the whole journey. Robots can be copied and services can be replicated, but an integrated system from design to construction is far harder to match.
